Overview: The Big Picture

Climate change is a major global issue that affects the planet’s climate, weather patterns, and natural environments. It is mainly caused by human activities such as burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and industrial production. Climate change is strongly connected to globalization because countries around the world are linked through trade, transportation, and economic activities that produce large amounts of greenhouse gas emissions. Its impacts—such as rising sea levels, stronger storms, and long periods of drought—are felt globally, affecting both developed and developing nations. Since the causes and effects of climate change cross national borders, countries must work together through global cooperation and shared solutions.

Cause and Effect

Causes

Fossil Fuel Combustion

Burning coal, oil, and gas for electricity and transport releases greenhouse gases.

Deforestation & Land Use

Clearing forests for agriculture and urban development reduces Earth's capacity to absorb CO2.

Industrial Agriculture

Large-scale farming, including livestock, releases significant amounts of methane and nitrous oxide.

Effects

Extreme Weather Events

Warmer oceans fuel stronger and more destructive typhoons, while other regions face severe droughts.

Sea Level Rise

Food Security Threats

Floods, droughts, and ocean acidification (coral bleaching) destroy crops and fisheries, impacting our food supply.

Filipino Context: Local Impact

Extreme Typhoon Vulnerability

The Philippines faces around 20 typhoons every year. In 2017, Bagyong Tino caused heavy rains, flooding, and damage in multiple regions—highlighting the country’s continued vulnerability to stronger and less predictable storms.

Impact on Agriculture

Changing weather patterns, including severe droughts and intense flooding, disrupt planting seasons and destroy crops, threatening the food security and livelihood of millions of Filipino farmers.

Sea Level Rise in Coastal Cities

Low-lying coastal areas, including parts of Metro Manila and Cebu, face a direct threat from rising sea levels, leading to more frequent flooding and saltwater intrusion into freshwater supplies.
